Crate Training an Adopted Adult French Bulldog That Refuses the Crate

Crate training an adopted adult French Bulldog starts with figuring out which part your dog refuses. A Frenchie that won't cross the threshold needs a different first lesson from one that naps inside until you touch the door. Check where the behavior changes before deciding what to practice or whether you need a crate for daily life.

Quick verdict: Start with an open crate and voluntary visits. Practice the door only after your dog can relax inside and leave freely. Use a familiar room or gated area for daily management if your dog settles there and the crate is still a training project.

What does refusing the crate actually look like?

Write down what happened just before your dog moved away. “I touched the latch and she stood up” gives you something to work with. “She hates the crate” lumps the bedding, entry, door, latch, and your departure into one problem. Start with the first change you can see and avoid repeating the hardest part to confirm it.

Ask the foster home what the dog used as a resting place, whether the door was ever closed, and whether anyone stayed nearby. A dog described as crate trained may have used a different crate in a different room. Ask what the behavior looked like instead of treating the label as a checklist you can skip. Build the exercise around the adult dog in front of you.

How do the options compare?

Choose the setup that solves today's problem. For a dog that won't approach, the exercise is a voluntary visit. For a household that needs somewhere for the dog to settle during deliveries, try a familiar area the dog already uses.

If all you need is a resting place, an open crate or a familiar bed may already solve the problem. For more ideas about what the dog can do in that space between sessions, see our guide to indoor enrichment for an adopted French Bulldog.

How do you make the first entry voluntary?

Battersea's crate training guide starts with the door secured open, enough room to stand, turn and lie down, and an invitation to investigate at the dog's pace. Set the crate up before bringing your Frenchie into the room. Keep the route in and out clear. Give them a familiar spot to rest elsewhere in the room so they have a real choice.

Start where your Frenchie can approach

If the dog stays across the room, leave the rewards on their side of the room. Mark a glance or a step toward the crate, then allow them to walk away. If they already sniff the entrance, reward that behavior. You can end a session there. Moving the reward straight to the back of the crate asks for entry and a deeper reach all at once.

Watch what happens after the reward

A dog may stretch forward, grab food, and back out before their rear paws cross the entry. Note what they do when the food is gone. An easy exit followed by a voluntary return tells you more about readiness than a single trip inside for a favorite treat. Keep the door still while you observe that difference.

Use an approach you've already practiced for a different new routine, such as getting used to the bath area. Work on entering, standing, and leaving before introducing the part that changes the experience. In the crate, that change is often the door or the person's movement away from it.

When should you move the door or step away?

Start door practice after the dog has chosen to stay and relax with the door fixed open. Dogs Trust's crate and pen guidance separates slight door movement from securing the door, and recommends returning to an easier stage if the dog shows worry. Stay where the dog can see you during the first closure exercises.

  1. Make one small door movement. Let the door open again immediately, with the exit available throughout. If your dog gets up to leave as soon as you reach for it, practice moving your hand toward the door from farther away instead.
  2. Practice the latch separately. Once door movement is familiar, briefly secure and reopen it while remaining beside the crate. If the sound changes the dog's behavior, back up to door movement without the latch.
  3. Change your position without extending the exercise. After the dog settles with you nearby, practice a small shift within view. Keep the rest of the setup familiar. Increase distance on a different repetition from any change in how long the door stays closed.
  4. Stop when your dog tries to leave. If your dog paws at the exit or tries to push out, end the exercise and use the last step they could do comfortably next time. Repeated attempts to escape are a reason to pause closed-door practice and ask a reward-based trainer or the rescue for help.

Keep crate practice separate from learning to spend time alone. Your dog may choose to rest in an open crate when you sit in the room and follow you when you leave. Check our guide to building a French Bulldog's home-alone routine for that second task.

What should you do when you need a setup today?

Use a familiar space where the dog has already rested. Start with an attended trial and observe the dog when you position a gate or move toward the doorway. If the barrier itself prompts repeated attempts to get through, try a different arrangement or get help from someone who can stay with the dog. Keep any necessary departure separate from a training experiment when you can.

Give household members the same instructions. For example: the crate stays open outside training, nobody moves its door while the dog rests, and the dog uses the familiar room during a delivery. Put the note somewhere everyone will see it. A shared plan prevents one person from turning a voluntary nap into a closure session.

Keep a simple practice log with the starting setup, the one thing you changed, the first response, and tomorrow's easier or repeatable step. “Door moved slightly, dog stood and exited, practice reaching toward the hinge next time” makes the next choice clearer than “Day four went badly.” Fit the exercise into a predictable day alongside your regular outdoor breaks.

Decide whose job each step is

In a busy household, give one person the job of keeping the practice log. Everyone can still use the same room routine. If someone needs to move the crate to clean, do it between sessions while the dog is elsewhere, then return it to its usual position. If a visitor is coming, choose the resting area before they arrive. That keeps the greeting and the crate lesson from becoming one event.

Which common shortcuts should you skip?

  • Shutting the door during a food lure. Let the dog finish and leave during open-door practice. Schedule door handling as its own exercise once resting is familiar.
  • Assuming a still dog is relaxed. Look for voluntary returns and whether the dog's usual resting posture changes when you move. Stop if the dog stiffens or makes for the exit.
  • Testing a full absence after a good rehearsal. Arrange your errand separately. A comfortable moment with the door closed and you beside it establishes that one condition.
  • Using the crate after a conflict. Preserve it as an optional resting place outside practice. If you need to redirect your dog during a busy moment, use a separate routine the dog knows.

Frequently asked questions

Can I crate train a French Bulldog adopted as an adult?

You can start by offering a fixed-open crate and rewarding voluntary interest. Use the dog’s current behavior to decide whether to work on approach, entry, or door movement. A familiar resting spot remains an option while you practice.

What if my Frenchie takes the treat and backs out?

Let them leave. Move the reward closer to the entrance and look for a relaxed return without calling them back. Practice entry and exit before asking for time inside.

Do I have to close the crate door?

If your goal is an optional resting place, you can leave it open. If you need brief closure for a specific routine, teach door movement separately once your dog chooses to relax inside.

What if my dog paws at the door?

End the exercise and return to an easier step next time. For repeated attempts to escape, pause closed-door work and consult the adoption organization or a reward-based trainer.

Does resting in the crate mean my dog can stay home alone?

Judge those situations separately. A dog may rest while you are present and get up when you leave. Build your distance from the crate in small steps and use an already familiar arrangement for absences.

What is the next step worth repeating?

Choose one thing your Frenchie can do comfortably, then keep the rest of the setup familiar. An approach, an easy exit, or a brief door movement can each be a complete lesson. If your dog already settles on a bed in a familiar room, keep that option available while you decide what crate practice adds.
