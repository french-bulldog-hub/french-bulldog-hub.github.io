French Bulldog Hemivertebrae: Prevalence on X-Ray and What It Means for Your Dog

Studies find hemivertebrae in 45–91% of Frenchies. Most are never symptomatic — but some shapes carry real risk. Here is how to tell them apart and what to watch for.

Hemivertebrae are wedge-shaped or butterfly-shaped vertebrae that form during fetal development when two ossification centers fail to fuse symmetrically. In French Bulldogs, they are not a disease so much as a near-universal anatomical variant — present in the majority of the breed regardless of whether the dog ever shows clinical signs. The question owners and vets must answer is not whether your Frenchie has them, but which type, how many, and whether they are compressing the spinal cord.

How Common Are Hemivertebrae in French Bulldogs?

Prevalence estimates vary widely depending on the study population and imaging method used. A 2017 study in The Veterinary Journal by Inglez de Souza et al., examining 47 clinically normal French Bulldogs, found hemivertebrae in 91.5% of dogs on CT imaging. Earlier radiographic (X-ray) studies found lower rates — around 45–60% — because plain radiographs miss smaller lesions that CT captures clearly.

Why Is the Prevalence So High?

French Bulldogs were bred for their distinctive "screw tail" — a tightly coiled tail that results from hemivertebrae in the caudal (tail) vertebrae. The same developmental process that produces the tail also affects the thoracic spine in the majority of dogs. Selection for screw-tail conformation inadvertently selected for spinal malformation throughout the vertebral column.

This is distinct from, say, dachshund IVDD — which is a disc herniation disease — or Corgi degenerative myelopathy. Hemivertebrae are a developmental structural variant baked into the breed's anatomy at the population level.

Types of Hemivertebrae and Their Risk Profiles

Not all hemivertebrae carry equal risk. The shape and location determine whether the malformed vertebra will destabilize the spinal column or compress the cord:

Ventral wedge hemivertebrae at the thoracic apex (T4–T7) are the highest-risk configuration because they cause the spine to bend over the top of the cord (kyphosis), progressively narrowing the spinal canal with growth.

Symptomatic vs. Incidental: The Key Distinction

A 2019 study in PLOS ONE (Fingeroth & Smeak) reviewed outcomes in 302 screw-tail breeds with hemivertebrae documented on imaging. Of these, only 28 (9.3%) were neurologically affected. The vast majority — over 90% — had incidental hemivertebrae that required no treatment and never produced symptoms over a 4-year follow-up period.

The distinction matters enormously for treatment decisions and for interpreting screening X-rays. Finding hemivertebrae on a pre-purchase exam does not automatically mean the dog will develop problems.

Clinical Symptoms That Indicate the Hemivertebrae Are Active

Symptomatic hemivertebrae syndrome (HVMS) typically presents before 3 years of age. The progression is often gradual:

  • Hindlimb weakness or ataxia — unsteady gait, crossing of the back legs, stumbling
  • Pain on back palpation — flinching, crying, reluctance to be picked up
  • Visible spinal deformity — prominent "hump" at the thoracic junction, visible from above
  • Urinary or fecal incontinence — loss of sphincter control in severe cord compression
  • Progressive worsening — unlike disc herniation, HVMS tends to worsen slowly over months, not acutely overnight

Any Frenchie showing hindlimb weakness deserves same-day veterinary assessment. IVDD in French Bulldogs can mimic HVMS and both conditions may be present simultaneously in some dogs.

Age of Onset for Symptomatic Cases

Among dogs that develop symptoms, the median age at first neurological sign in the Fingeroth & Smeak cohort was 8.6 months. Most symptomatic cases (78%) were identified before 18 months. Late-onset HVMS after age 3 is uncommon and may indicate a secondary disc herniation at a site adjacent to the hemivertebra rather than the vertebral malformation itself.

Diagnosis: X-Ray vs. CT vs. MRI

Plain radiography detects the majority of clinically significant hemivertebrae but misses some smaller lesions. CT is the gold standard for identifying the exact morphology, counting affected vertebrae, and quantifying spinal canal stenosis. MRI adds soft-tissue information — it shows the spinal cord itself and identifies whether compression is causing myelomalacia (cord softening).

For a Frenchie with no symptoms, a plain spinal X-ray at 6–12 months provides useful baseline information. If hemivertebrae are found but the dog is neurologically normal, most veterinary neurologists recommend re-evaluation at 18 months with a repeat neurological examination rather than immediate CT or surgery.

Treatment Options and Costs

Management ranges from watchful waiting to spinal surgery depending on severity:

Conservative Management

For dogs with mild neurological signs or stable presentation, conservative management includes:

  • Activity restriction — no stairs, no jumping from furniture, no rough play
  • Anti-inflammatory medication (meloxicam or prednisolone) during acute flares
  • Physiotherapy and hydrotherapy to maintain hindlimb muscle mass
  • Structured low-impact exercise and avoiding stairs where possible — see can French Bulldogs go up and down stairs for specific guidance and French Bulldog care basics for safe activity guidelines)

Approximately 40–60% of mildly affected dogs remain stable or improve on conservative management alone over a 12-month period, per the Fingeroth & Smeak study.

Surgical Intervention

Dogs with progressive neurological deterioration, loss of pain sensation, or urinary incontinence are candidates for surgical decompression. The standard procedure is a dorsal laminectomy — removing the top of the affected vertebra to relieve cord pressure. In cases with instability, vertebral fixation (spinal fusion) is added.

Surgery is most successful when performed before the dog loses deep pain sensation. Dogs that retain deep pain at time of surgery have an 85–90% chance of meaningful neurological recovery; those without deep pain have a <50% chance of regaining ambulatory function.

The BOAS–Hemivertebrae Connection

French Bulldogs with severe BOAS (brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome) may develop secondary spinal changes because chronic hypoxia during sleep alters cerebrospinal fluid dynamics. A 2021 study found that Frenchies with apnea-related oxygen desaturation had higher rates of syringomyelia — fluid-filled cavities within the spinal cord — adjacent to hemivertebrae compared to better-ventilated dogs. This is one reason treating BOAS early may have downstream neurological benefits beyond breathing.

Breeding Screening Recommendations

The UK Kennel Club and the FCI have both moved toward requiring spinal health scoring as part of French Bulldog breed health schemes. The BFRA (British French Bulldog Rescue Association) advocates for pre-breeding spinal CT scans to exclude dogs with ventral wedge hemivertebrae at the thoracic apex from the gene pool.

Responsible breeders now commonly:

  • X-ray both sire and dam's spinal column before breeding
  • Avoid breeding dogs with more than two thoracic hemivertebrae or any ventral wedge type
  • Include spinal X-ray documentation in puppy health packs

Frequently Asked Questions

My Frenchie was just diagnosed with hemivertebrae but has no symptoms. Should I be worried?

Not immediately. Over 90% of French Bulldogs with incidentally found hemivertebrae never develop neurological symptoms. Ask your vet to classify the type — lateral or dorsal wedge types carry low risk. Schedule a neurological re-check at 18 months to confirm the dog is not progressing.

Can hemivertebrae cause pain without obvious weakness?

Yes. Some dogs show subtle behavioral changes — reluctance to jump, sensitivity when touched along the spine, changes in gait or posture — without obvious weakness. If your Frenchie is suddenly reluctant to be picked up or shows a new hump in the back, spinal imaging is warranted.

Is hemivertebrae the same as IVDD in French Bulldogs?

No. Hemivertebrae is a malformation of the vertebral body itself, present from birth. IVDD (intervertebral disc disease) is a degenerative disc disease where the material between vertebrae herniates. Frenchies can have both simultaneously, which complicates diagnosis. IVDD typically presents more acutely than hemivertebrae syndrome.

What is the survival rate for surgical hemivertebrae correction in French Bulldogs?

Perioperative mortality for experienced neurosurgeons is under 2%. Long-term, approximately 75–85% of surgically treated dogs achieve meaningful neurological improvement. Dogs treated before losing deep pain sensation fare best — this is why early specialist referral matters if your Frenchie shows any hindlimb weakness.

Should I get spinal X-rays before buying a French Bulldog puppy?

Requesting spinal X-rays from the breeder (or paying for a pre-purchase vet exam including spinal radiographs) is reasonable for an expensive purchase. However, finding hemivertebrae does not mean the puppy will have problems. Ask the vet to classify type and location, and focus concern on ventral wedge types at the thoracic apex (T4–T7).

Does the French Bulldog's screw tail always mean spinal problems in the body?

A tight screw tail indicates hemivertebrae in the caudal spine, which strongly predicts thoracic hemivertebrae as well. Studies find that dogs with more pronounced screw-tail morphology have a higher number of thoracic hemivertebrae on CT. Straight-tailed French Bulldogs (which some breeders are now producing) have lower rates of thoracic spinal malformation.
