French Bulldog Buyer Warning: BOAS, EU pressure, and why puppy scams are rising

The French Bulldog is currently one of the most popular companion dogs in the world, yet it is also one of the most medically compromised breeds in existence…

Home›Blog›French Bulldog Buyer Warning: BOAS, EU pressure, and why puppy scams are rising

Published: 2026-05-22 · French Bulldog Hub

The French Bulldog is currently one of the most popular companion dogs in the world, yet it is also one of the most medically compromised breeds in existence. The European Union is now moving toward tighter restrictions on breeding and importation of dogs with Brachycephalic Obstructive Airway Syndrome (BOAS), and this regulatory shift has opened a new window for puppy scammers. If you are looking for a French Bulldog in 2025, understanding the intersection of BOAS legislation, rising fraud, and ethical breeding is not optional—it is essential.

The United Kingdom and several EU member states have already introduced or are drafting legislation that specifically targets extreme brachycephaly. The Dutch government, for example, has considered outright bans on importing dogs with a muzzle length below a certain ratio—essentially targeting many French Bulldogs. As pressure from the European Union mounts, the market for "health-tested" or "BOAS-free" French Bulldogs is skyrocketing. This has created a perfect storm for scammers who exploit buyer desperation by offering papers and health clearances that do not exist. On this page, I will walk you through exactly what BOAS is, how the EU ban is shifting the market, how to identify a puppy scam, and how to buy responsibly. What is BOAS and why does it matter?

Defining Brachycephalic Obstructive Airway Syndrome

Brachycephalic Obstructive Airway Syndrome, or BOAS, is a pathological condition caused by selective breeding for short muzzles. According to the Merck Veterinary Manual, BOAS includes anatomical abnormalities such as stenotic nares (pinched nostrils), an elongated soft palate, everted laryngeal saccules, and a narrow trachea. These structural defects obstruct airflow and cause chronic respiratory distress. A 2020 study published in PLOS ONE found that over 50% of French Bulldogs examined in a UK veterinary teaching hospital met the clinical criteria for BOAS. The PubMed literature consistently shows that French Bulldogs are disproportionately affected compared to other brachycephalic breeds.

The severity of BOAS is graded from 0 (unaffected) to 3 (severe). Dogs with grade 2 or 3 often require surgical intervention and may suffer from heat intolerance, exercise collapse, and sleep apnea. Because the condition is directly linked to skull shape, breeding dogs with moderate to severe BOAS is now considered unethical by many veterinary associations.

How BOAS affects daily life

French Bulldogs with BOAS struggle to pant effectively. Since dogs cannot sweat like humans, panting is their primary cooling mechanism. A dog with stenotic nares cannot move enough air to regulate body temperature, putting them at high risk for heatstroke even after mild exertion. Many owners report that their French Bulldog cannot walk more than 10 minutes without collapsing or breathing heavily. Surgical correction can improve quality of life but does not eliminate the underlying conformational defect. The EU legislative shift against BOAS

Current legislation and proposed bans

The European Union does not yet have a single, uniform ban on the sale of brachycephalic dogs, but several member states have taken significant action. The Netherlands, for example, has banned the breeding of dogs with a muzzle length shorter than one-third of the head length. In 2023, the Dutch government also proposed restrictions on importing brachycephalic dogs from outside the EU. The UK, while no longer part of the EU, has its own restrictions: under the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (England) Regulations 2018, breeders cannot license a dog if breeding it could cause welfare problems—and many French Bulldogs fall into this category.

In 2024, the European Commission began reviewing a proposal to standardize breeding restrictions across all member states. The goal is to prevent "welfare tourism," where breeders move dogs from countries with no restrictions to EU markets. If passed, importation of French Bulldogs without certified BOAS testing may be illegal across 27 nations.

Market implications of the ban

The pressure from the EU has had two effects. First, demand for "BOAS-tested" or "breathing-friendly" French Bulldogs has surged. Some breeders advertise dogs that have passed a functional BOAS test, which measures oxygen saturation and exercise tolerance. Second, scammers now use the term "BOAS-free" as a marketing gimmick, often providing fake certificates from nonexistent organizations. Because buyers are frantic to find a dog that will not be subject to future import restrictions, they are more likely to hand over deposits without proper verification. Why puppy scams are rising

The anatomy of a modern puppy scam

Puppy scammers have always existed, but the EU BOAS ban has given them a new narrative. A typical scam begins with a website or social media page showing adorable French Bulldog puppies at impossibly low prices. The seller claims the puppies are "EU-compliant," "BOAS-grade 0," and fully vaccinated. They ask for a deposit via wire transfer, Zelle, or cryptocurrency. Once payment is sent, the seller invents problems: shipping insurance, extra customs fees, quarantine costs. Each request is another wire transfer. Eventually, the seller disappears. The American Kennel Club reports that puppy scams have increased by over 30% since 2022, with French Bulldogs being the most-targeted breed due to their high price point (average $3,000–$8,000).

How scammers use the EU ban as leverage

Scammers now email or message buyers claiming that due to the "upcoming EU ban," they must move quickly to secure a puppy before importation becomes illegal. This urgency eliminates the buyer's natural caution. They might also show fake certificates that look like official BOAS screening results from a veterinary university or European agency. Some scammers even impersonate real breeders by copying their AKC registration numbers. How to verify a breeder's BOAS claims

Request specific health testing documentation

A legitimate breeder will provide a BOAS respiratory function grading certificate from an independent institution. In Europe, the most recognized program is the Brachycephalic Breed Respiratory Function Grading Scheme, run by the University of Cambridge and the Kennel Club. In the US, the OFA offers a BOAS certification. Every dog should have an individual certificate, not just a breeder's blanket statement. Also request AKC registration or equivalent registry papers, hip scores (OFA or PennHIP), and cardiac clearance.

Video verification and third-party checks

Ask for a live video call where you see the puppy with its mother and the environment. Scammers will almost always refuse, claiming the cameras are broken or the breeder is "busy." A responsible breeder welcomes video calls because they want to ensure their puppy goes to a good home. If you are buying from outside your country, consider hiring a local pet transport company or a veterinary student to physically inspect the situation. Red flags in online French Bulldog listings

Price and payment

If the price is below $2,500 for a French Bulldog puppy in 2025, it is almost certainly a red flag. Realistic prices for a well-bred, health-tested French Bulldog range from $4,000 to $10,000. Scammers often list puppies for $1,200 or $1,500 to lure budget-conscious buyers. They only accept payment methods that cannot be reversed: bank wire transfer, Zelle, CashApp, or gift cards. Credit cards and PayPal Goods & Services offer buyer protection—scammers avoid them. If you encounter these tactics, report the listing and visit our contact page to alert our team.

Photos and description inconsistencies

Reverse image search the photos. Scammers frequently steal images from legitimate breeders' websites or Instagram accounts. Look for low-resolution photos, watermarks from other sites, or images that look professionally produced but are not accompanied by specific details about the puppy. Legitimate breeders will have multiple photos of the same puppy at different angles, in different lighting, and over a period of weeks.

How the EU ban affects buyers in the United States

While the EU ban directly affects European buyers, it has a ripple effect globally. American buyers are increasingly targeted by international puppy scams claiming that a dog is "stuck" at customs due to new EU restrictions. In reality, the US has no import ban on French Bulldogs, but scammers fabricate one to collect fake customs fees. If you live in the US and a seller claims you need to pay for "EU compliance shipping" or "BOAS import clearance," it is a fraud. You can verify import regulations on the USDA APHIS website. What to do if you suspect a scam

Report immediately

If you have sent money to a suspected scammer, contact your bank immediately to attempt a chargeback or reverse the wire. File a report with the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) in the US, Action Fraud in the UK, or your local consumer protection agency. Also report the breeder's website to hosting providers and social media platforms.

Join legitimate communities

There are reputable French Bulldog clubs and rescue organizations that maintain lists of tested breeders. The AKC's health resources are an excellent starting point. You can also contact veterinary schools or the Orthopedic Foundation for Animals to verify health clearances. For community support, see our about page.

The future of the breed: breeding for health

Several progressive breeding programs in Europe and North America are now selecting for longer muzzles and open nostrils. This is controversial among traditional show breeders, but the data supports it: dogs with slightly longer muzzles live longer, breathe better, and still look recognizably French Bulldog. The Dutch Kennel Club has already recognized a "retro-mop" type that retains the breed's characteristic bat ears and compact body but with a functional muzzle length. As the EU ban takes hold, these healthier lines will be the only ones that can be legally imported or bred. Supporting these ethical breeders ensures the long-term survival of the breed. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What does BOAS stand for?

BOAS stands for Brachycephalic Obstructive Airway Syndrome, a respiratory condition common in flat-faced dog breeds like the French Bulldog.

Is the EU banning French Bulldogs?

The EU has not banned the breed outright, but several member states have introduced restrictions on breeding and importing dogs with extreme brachycephaly.

How can I tell if a French Bulldog has BOAS?

Signs include noisy breathing, snoring, exercise intolerance, gagging, and collapsing after mild exercise. Only a veterinary respiratory function test can confirm BOAS grade.

What is a BOAS respiratory function test?

It is a clinical test that measures oxygen saturation, exercise tolerance, and anatomical obstruction. The grading scale goes from 0 (unaffected) to 3 (severe).

Are all French Bulldogs affected by BOAS?

Studies show that over 50% of French Bulldogs in veterinary settings have clinical signs of BOAS, but prevalence varies by lineage.

What is the average price of a healthy French Bulldog in 2025?

Expect to pay between $4,000 and $10,000 for a health-tested French Bulldog from an ethical breeder.

How do I avoid a French Bulldog puppy scam?

Always request a live video call, reverse image search photos, verify health clearances through public databases, and never pay via wire transfer or gift cards.

What if a breeder says their dog is "BOAS-free"?

The term is misleading—no dog can be 100% free of risk due to conformation. Request a numerical BOAS grade from OFA or an equivalent independent agency.

Can a French Bulldog live a normal life with BOAS?

With mild BOAS and proper management (weight control, no strenuous exercise in heat), many dogs have good quality of life. Severe BOAS may require surgery.

What is the EU doing about puppy scams?

The EU has launched consumer awareness campaigns and is working on legislation to standardize breeding restrictions, but enforcement remains uneven across member states.

Where can I find verified health clearances for a French Bulldog?

Check the public databases of the Orthopedic Foundation for Animals (OFA), the Kennel Club in the UK, or university respiratory function grading programs.

What are stenotic nares?

Stenotic nares are abnormally narrow nostrils, one of the anatomical abnormalities that contribute to BOAS in French Bulldogs.

Do rescue French Bulldogs have BOAS?

Many rescue French Bulldogs have some degree of BOAS. Rescue organizations should be transparent about respiratory issues and veterinary assessments.

Is pet insurance important for a French Bulldog?

Yes. Given the breed's high risk for BOAS surgery, skin infections, and joint problems, pet insurance is strongly recommended to manage long-term medical costs.

What is the difference between OFA and PennHIP?

OFA hip evaluation requires the dog to be at least 24 months old and uses a subjective grade, while PennHIP is a more precise measurement taken at any age. Both are credible.
