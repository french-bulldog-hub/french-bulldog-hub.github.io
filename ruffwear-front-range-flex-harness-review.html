Ruffwear Front Range Flex Harness Review 2026: Sizing and Fit on a French Bulldog's Deep Chest and Wide Shoulders

A hands-on sizing guide to Ruffwear's knit everyday harness: which size band a Frenchie actually lands in, where the Flex flatters a barrel chest, and the fit checks that decide whether it stays.

Frenchie Hub Content Team

The Ruffwear Front Range Flex Harness is the lighter, softer sibling of the harness most Frenchie owners already know, and on a French Bulldog the question is never really about the knit. It is about whether a harness built from a girth chart can wrap a chest that is deep, wide and set low on short legs, then hold still on a waist that is narrower than the ribs above it. This review walks through the 2026 size bands, the points of adjustment, and the moments where a Frenchie's shape turns a straightforward size chart into a two-size decision.

Quick verdict: Most adult French Bulldogs measure roughly 17 to 26 inches (43 to 65 cm) around the chest, which puts them in the XS or Small band of the Flex chart. Dogs at the smaller end, around 17 to 22 inches (43 to 56 cm), start in XS; dogs from 22 to 27 inches (56 to 69 cm) start in Small. The Flex retails at $69.99 on Ruffwear's own product page and carries a 4.7 average from 89 reviews there, while the original Front Range is $59.99 with a 4.8 average from 4,265 reviews.

Frenchie harness fit checker: which Flex size do you start with?

Measure your dog standing square, put the tape around the widest part of the ribcage just behind the front legs, and let the checker translate that number into a Ruffwear band plus the red flags that matter on a deep-chested, wide-shouldered dog.

Flex size and fit checker

The checker uses the six girth bands Ruffwear publishes for the Front Range Flex: 9 to 13 inches for XXXS, 13 to 17 for XXS, 17 to 22 for XS, 22 to 27 for Small, 27 to 32 for Medium and 32 to 42 for Large/XLarge. Ruffwear measures around the widest part of the ribcage and tells owners who sit between sizes to choose the bigger one, which is the rule the tool follows, with an extra nudge for wide shoulders.

The clip below is Ruffwear's own 55-second demonstration of where the tape goes: around the widest point of the ribcage, flat and snug, with the dog standing normally rather than sitting.

What the Flex changes compared with the original Front Range

The Flex keeps the layout that made the Front Range a default city harness and changes the material story. The body is a 75% polyester, 25% nylon engineered knit rather than cut-and-sew panels, so there are fewer seams to sit on a Frenchie's ribs, and the harness still uses four points of adjustment and side-release buckles for on and off.

The hardware is the part that matters on a strong little dog. The back attachment is an anodized 6061-T6 aluminium V-ring, the chest attachment is reinforced nylon webbing in a moulded TPU piece, and the buckles are ITW Nexus Mach 1 side-release units that Ruffwear lists as bluesign approved. A reflective trim strip and an integrated light loop for Ruffwear's Beacon round out the low-light kit, and there is a small ID pocket for tags. The Flex is made in Vietnam.

The headline difference for Frenchie owners is the new XXXS band. The original Front Range starts at XXS and stops there, which leaves a small puppy or a very compact adult at the very bottom of the chart. The Flex adds 9 to 13 inches (23 to 33 cm), so a young Frenchie has a band of its own instead of wearing a harness two sizes too big while it grows.

The Flex size chart in French Bulldog terms

A working estimate for adult Frenchies is 45 to 65 cm (about 17.7 to 25.6 inches) around the chest, with weights around 11 to 12.5 kg. That range lands entirely in XS and Small on the Flex chart, which is why most owners never need to look at Medium unless their dog is unusually large for the breed.

Weight is a poor proxy for harness size on this breed, and the breed numbers explain why. The FCI standard summarised on Wikipedia's French Bulldog page gives males 9 to 14 kg (20 to 31 lb) and females 8 to 13 kg (18 to 29 lb), while the AKC standard caps the breed at 28 lb. Two dogs can share a weight and sit in different bands, because the difference is chest volume, not mass. One owner on the French Bulldog Forum's harness-fitting thread reports a Front Range in Small fitting a 24 to 25 lb boy, which is a useful reality check against the chart: a Small is plausible at that weight, but so is an XS on a narrower dog.

Deep chest, wide shoulders: where the Flex helps and where it fights back

A Frenchie's chest is wide, deep and rounded like a barrel, and the waist behind it is noticeably narrower. That shape has two consequences for any harness, as the FitMyPet French Bulldog harness guide points out: the chest measurement is large for such a short dog, and a loose harness can slide backwards over the ribs because there is less body behind the chest to stop it.

The Flex's four adjustment points are the answer to the second problem. You can take in the girth straps until the body of the harness sits snug behind the front legs, then open the neck straps so the front of the harness is not pressing on the throat. On a deep-chested dog, those two adjustments are not cosmetic: the girth strap does most of the holding, and the neck opening only needs to be wide enough to clear the chest without sliding forward.

Wide shoulders are the harder test. Ruffwear's size bands describe the harness at its loosest and tightest settings rather than at one fixed measurement, according to the fitting guide from retailer Mountain Dog, which is why a dog can be "in" a band and still be at the very end of its strap travel. If your Frenchie's girth sits in the top inch of a band, the harness will go on, but most of the adjustment is used up before the dog has even moved. That is the case where ordering the next size up, or both, is the sensible move.

There is one more fit variable worth watching on any harness: the front of the harness sits over the shoulder, and a tight or badly placed strap can change how far the shoulder reaches forward. FitMyPet cites a small study finding that harnesses can shorten shoulder extension at walk and trot, and suggests watching your dog's stride against its unharnessed stride. If the front legs look choppier with the harness on, the fit needs work before the harness becomes the default walking gear.

How to measure a Frenchie for the Flex

Stand the dog on a level floor with all four feet square underneath it. Wrap a soft tape around the widest part of the ribcage, just behind the front legs, keeping it flat and untwisted and snug against the short coat without squeezing. Read the number where the end meets the tape, then measure a second time; if the readings differ, use the larger one.

Measure the neck at the base, where a collar would sit, and write both numbers down. The Giddy Pets sizing guide makes the same point about this breed in different words: a harness sized correctly for chest girth can still be wrong around the neck, and vice versa, because the neck is short and thick relative to the body.

Then apply the Ruffwear rule. Between two sizes, take the larger one, and do the same if your dog is on a border and fully grown. The exception is a dog whose larger size would only close on its tightest setting: FitMyPet argues that on a deep-chested, narrow-waisted dog, a smaller size that fits properly on the chest is safer than a larger one that can be backed out of. In practice, that means trying both when your measurement lands within half an inch of a band edge.

Puppies get their own rule: measure today's chest, not the adult chest you expect. A Frenchie's chest keeps broadening as it matures, so a harness bought to grow into will be loose now, and a loose harness on a barrel chest slips and rubs. Re-measure every couple of weeks while the dog is growing fast.

Fit checks once the harness is on

The two-finger test is the fastest check and the one most brands agree on: you should be able to slide two fingers snugly under every strap without forcing them in. The Giddy Pets guide also flags the failure mode specific to this breed, which is a harness that fits the chest but has a neck opening too big for a short, thick neck, so the whole thing twists to one side mid-walk.

Work through this list on the first walk:

  • Strap position. The body strap should sit a little way behind the front legs, not pressed up into the armpit where it will rub with every stride.
  • Throat clearance. Every strap at the front should sit on the breastbone or low on the chest, never across the throat.
  • Shoulder freedom. Watch the dog walk and trot. The stride should look like the stride without the harness.
  • Escape test. With the harness on, gently pull the lead forwards, backwards and upwards. It should not slide over the shoulders or the ribs. If it does, tighten the girth strap or accept that this shape of harness is not the right one.
  • Rubbing check. After the first few walks, look behind the front legs, under the chest and around the neck for redness or broken hairs. Loosen or reposition before the next walk.
  • Re-check after a week. Webbing and buckles settle in the first few walks, so run the two-finger test again.

The Flex makes the rubbing check easier in one respect and harder in another. The knit body has fewer seams to dig in, but because the material is soft it also shows strap tension less obviously than a padded shell, so it is worth physically sliding a finger under the girth strap rather than judging by eye.

Everyday use: city walks, cars and stairs

For an apartment Frenchie, the Flex does the job it was designed for: a light everyday harness with a back ring for normal walks and a chest ring for the days your dog leans into the lead. The chest attachment redirects forward pull rather than stopping it, so it helps a dog that already walks reasonably and gives you a second option on a busy pavement.

The reflective trim and light loop are the details that earn their place in a city. A short winter walk at 5 pm is exactly when a low-slung, dark-coated dog disappears against a kerb, and a clip-on light on the loop costs nothing in weight. If you drive to your walking spot, a harness is not a car restraint, and the French Bulldog car seat guide covers the booster-and-tether setup that keeps a Frenchie in place on the road.

Harness fit also interacts with how far you walk. A harness that rubs turns a twenty-minute loop into a battle, and Frenchies are not a breed that needs long distances anyway; our guide to how far a French Bulldog can walk sets realistic distances for the breed. If your route includes a stairwell, the harness handle-free design of the Flex means you will still carry or use a ramp on the flights your dog has not learned, which our stair safety guide covers in detail.

None of this replaces basic fit hygiene. A harness is only as good as the measurement behind it, and the breed's real weight range is wider than most owners expect, as our breakdown of real Frenchie weights versus the AKC 28 lb figure shows. If you are still assembling the basics, the care basics guide puts gear in the context of the rest of the routine.

Pros and cons for a French Bulldog

What works

  • The knit body is soft against short coats and has fewer seams to rub behind the front legs.
  • Four points of adjustment let you fit the chest snugly and still open the neck clear of the throat.
  • Two leash points, including a reinforced chest loop for a dog that pulls.
  • The new XXXS band gives puppies and very small adults a size that actually fits.
  • Reflective trim, a light loop and an ID pocket are all useful in a city, not filler.
  • Aluminium V-ring and name-brand side-release buckles are the right hardware for a strong small dog.

What to weigh up

  • It costs $10 more than the original Front Range at Ruffwear's list price.
  • It has a much shorter review record, 89 reviews against 4,265 for the original, so the long-term picture is thinner.
  • A Frenchie sits in the middle bands, where a measurement half an inch off changes the size you order.
  • The soft knit hides a too-tight girth strap, so the fit check has to be deliberate rather than visual.
  • If your dog needs a handle or a lift assist, the Flex is not that harness.

Frequently asked questions

What size Front Range Flex does a French Bulldog wear?

Most adult French Bulldogs measure about 17 to 26 inches (43 to 65 cm) around the chest, which puts them in XS (17–22 in) or Small (22–27 in). Measure the widest part of the ribcage behind the front legs and use that number, not the dog's weight.

Is the Flex better than the original Front Range for a Frenchie?

It depends on the dog. The Flex adds a soft knit body and a new XXXS band, but costs $69.99 against $59.99 and has far fewer reviews. The original has the longer track record and the same four-point adjustment layout.

My Frenchie is between XS and Small. Which should I order?

Ruffwear's rule is to choose the larger size when a fully grown dog sits between sizes, and the Flex chart supports that. The exception is a dog whose larger size would only fit on its tightest setting; then a properly fitted smaller size is the safer choice on a deep-chested breed.

Why does my Frenchie's harness twist to one side?

Usually because the harness is too loose around the chest, or the neck opening is too big for a short, thick neck. Recheck both measurements against the Flex chart, tighten the girth straps to the two-finger fit, and reset the neck straps before deciding the size is wrong.

Can a French Bulldog back out of the Front Range Flex?

Any harness can be backed out of if it is loose, and a Frenchie's narrower waist makes that easier than on a deep-chested breed with a fuller ribcage. Test it by gently pulling the lead forwards, backwards and upwards; if the harness slides over the ribs, tighten it or choose a design with a second strap behind the ribcage.

Does the Flex work for a Frenchie puppy?

The XXXS band, 9 to 13 inches, gives small puppies a proper starting size that the original Front Range did not offer. Measure the chest every couple of weeks while the puppy grows and move up when the measurement reaches the top of the current band rather than buying a size to grow into.

Is a harness better than a collar for walks?

Many owners and trainers prefer a harness for flat-faced breeds because it keeps pressure off the neck, and the Giddy Pets guide notes that this is why a well-fitted harness is generally preferred for French Bulldogs. A collar is still useful for ID tags.

How tight should the girth strap be on the Flex?

Snug enough that two fingers slide under the strap without forcing, and tight enough that the harness does not rotate when you pull the lead sideways. Check behind the front legs for redness after the first few walks and loosen one setting if you find any.

The verdict

The Front Range Flex is a good harness for a French Bulldog, with one condition: the size has to come from a tape measure, not a weight chart. The XS and Small bands cover almost the entire breed, the four-point adjustment gives you enough range to fit a barrel chest and keep the neck opening clear, and the knit body is genuinely kinder to a short coat than a stiffer padded shell. What it does not do is solve the breed's core fitting problem for you. A Frenchie whose girth sits at the top of a band, whose shoulders are wide, and whose waist is narrow is still a two-size decision, and the Flex still ends up on the tightest or loosest end of its straps if you guess.

If you already own the original Front Range and it fits, there is no urgent reason to switch: the layout is the same and the original is cheaper with a much longer review record. If you are buying for a puppy, a very small adult, or a dog that has been rubbing behind the front legs in a stiffer harness, the Flex's softer body and the new XXXS band make it the more sensible starting point. Measure, order the band your tape says, and run the fit checks before the first long walk.
